# How ParentVeda talks in Trying to Conceive — the voice guide

Written 2026-09-25 for the TTC warmth pass (`docs/TTC-WARMTH-PASS.md`). Every piece of text in the TTC stage is
rewritten to this guide: reads, door tiles, daily cards, tools, buttons, empty screens, disclaimers. **Status: APPROVED by the user
2026-09-26** ("the language needs to be simple… in a way the human can easily understand in one go. Don't flex
your English"). Applied to the whole TTC stage the same day.

---

## 1. Who we are talking to

A woman, often in India, who has been trying for a baby for some months. She may be hopeful, tired, anxious or
quietly sad, and she may be reading this at night with no one to ask. Sometimes her partner is reading too.

We sound like **a kind friend who happens to know the medicine**. We are warm and calm, we tell her the truth, and
we are on her side. We are not a textbook, a lecturer or a clever essay.

## 2. The rules

1. **Talk to her as "you".** Use "your partner" or "you both" when it is about the couple. Never "a woman" or "she"
   when we mean the reader.
2. **Use everyday contractions:** it's, you're, don't, isn't, we'll. This one change does the most to stop the text
   sounding stiff.
3. **Short sentences, one idea each.** Aim for about 15 words per sentence on average. Keep paragraphs to two or
   three sentences.
4. **Plain words first.** If a medical word is needed, say what it means the first time ("LH, the hormone that
   triggers ovulation"). Titles say what is inside, in the words she would type into a search.
5. **Name the feeling once, then help.** One short line that shows we understand ("This part can feel like a lot"),
   then straight into something useful. Never a paragraph of sympathy, and never "we understand how you feel".
6. **Reassure with facts, not cheerleading.** "This is very common" or "Most couples take several months" comforts
   her. "You've got this!" does not.
7. **Never make her feel at fault.** Leave out anything that hints she caused a bad month. Myths are corrected
   gently, because the person who told her usually loves her.
8. **No dashes.** No em dashes (—) and no spaced hyphens used as dashes ( - ). Use a full stop, a comma, a colon or
   brackets. Ranges are written with "to": "21 to 35 days". Ordinary hyphenated words (fertility-friendly) are fine.
   Two exceptions: a compact date range in a tight one-line row keeps an en dash ("3 Sep – 8 Sep"), because "to"
   overflows at phone width; and a blank-field placeholder may be a single dash.
9. **No CAPITALS for emphasis** ("the days BEFORE ovulation"). Put the important word where it lands naturally,
   usually at the end of the sentence.
10. **No clever lines.** No punchlines, twists or "it's not X, it's Y" sayings. They read as written by a machine
    trying to sound wise.
11. **Doctor lines stay calm and specific.** Say exactly when to see someone and how soon, without alarm. Keep every
    "today" or "straight away" exactly as urgent as it was.
12. **Screen text is short and friendly.** Buttons are verbs ("Log today", "See my window"). Empty screens invite
    her in and say what she'll get. Error messages say what happened and what to do, and never blame her.
13. **Keep it Indian where it helps.** Chemist, sister-in-law, rupees, Indian foods. No American-only references.
14. **About the same length.** A rewrite should be within about 15% of the original's length. Warmer does not
    mean longer.

## 3. What must never change

- **Facts.** Numbers, doses, timings, day counts, ages, sources, and warning signs stay exactly as they are.
- **Safety.** Every "see a doctor" line, disclaimer and "this is not a diagnosis" keeps its full meaning and
  urgency. We never give her a personal chance of success, and we never contradict her own doctor.
- **Anything the code relies on.** Ids, keys, saved values and names the app compares are not text for her and
  stay untouched.
- **The Hindi side** of existing pairs stays as it is (new work is English).
- **Other apps' words.** Flo and What to Expect show us the tone. We never copy their sentences. Every rewritten
  batch is checked against their text (see the plagiarism check in the ledger).

## 4. The banned list

These make text sound machine-made, preachy or falsely cheerful. The rewrite must not contain them.

**Words and phrases**
- journey (as a metaphor), navigate, empower, delve, embark, holistic (unless literal), game-changer, crucial
- you've got this, rest assured, we understand how you feel, remember (as an opener)
- it's important to note, it's worth noting, let's dive in, in today's world
- genuinely, actually, quietly, simply, truly (as filler words)
- "the single most", "the one thing nobody tells you", "here's the thing"

**Habits**
- exclamation marks and emoji
- piles of questions at the start ("Wondering…? Worried…?")
- lists of three everywhere ("calm, clear and kind")
- dashes of any kind as punctuation (rule 8)
- capitals for emphasis (rule 9)
- punchlines and wise-sounding sayings (rule 10)
- jokes or wry remarks at painful moments (loss, a negative test, a hard result)
- scolding or mocking the advice her family gave

## 5. Before and after, from our own app

Each pair shows the rules at work. The "after" says the same thing, with the same facts.

**1. Opening a read** (How conception actually works)
> Before: A menstrual cycle is not one process. It is two, running back to back, and they behave completely
> differently — which is the single most useful thing to know about it, and the thing almost nobody is taught.
>
> After: Your cycle is really two halves, one after the other, and they behave very differently. Most of us were
> never taught this. Once you know it, a lot of the confusing parts start to make sense.

**2. The line under the title** (same read)
> Before: Almost nothing about conception happens on a schedule you can see. Understanding the mechanism will not
> make it happen faster — but it turns a month of guessing into a month you can read, and that is most of what makes
> the waiting bearable.
>
> After: Most of what happens when you're trying to conceive happens out of sight. Knowing how it works won't make
> it happen faster. It can turn a month of guessing into a month that makes sense, and that makes the wait a little
> easier.

**3. Correcting a myth** (stress)
> Before: Severe, sustained stress can delay or stop ovulation, and that is real. Ordinary stress — work, a
> difficult month, worrying about this very question — has not been shown to prevent conception. This myth deserves
> particular impatience, because it hands a woman a reason to blame herself for something she cannot control.
>
> After: Very high stress that goes on for a long time can delay or stop ovulation, and that's real. Everyday
> stress, like a hard month at work or worrying about this, hasn't been shown to stop you getting pregnant. So if
> someone tells you to just relax, please don't hear it as your fault. It isn't.

**4. A question and answer** (best time of day)
> Before: No. Sperm counts vary slightly through the day and the difference is far too small to plan around. Anyone
> selling you a time of day is selling you something.
>
> After: No. Sperm counts change a little through the day, but not enough to matter. Whatever time suits the two of
> you is the right time.

**5. When to see a doctor** (cycle length)
> Before: If your cycles are shorter than 21 days or longer than 35, if they swing widely from month to month, if
> periods have stopped for three months or more, or if bleeding is heavy enough to interfere with your day. None of
> these is an emergency, and all of them are far easier to investigate with two or three months of dates written
> down — so start the record now and book when it suits you.
>
> After: It's worth seeing a doctor if your cycles are shorter than 21 days or longer than 35, if they change a lot
> from month to month, if your periods have stopped for three months or more, or if bleeding is heavy enough to get
> in the way of your day. None of these is an emergency. Two or three months of dates will help your doctor a lot,
> so start noting them now and book a visit when it suits you.

**6. A tip box** (tracking)
> Before: You can stop. A couple having sex every two days through the middle of the cycle covers the window
> without a single strip, chart or app notification. Tracking is useful when cycles are irregular or when you want
> data for a doctor — it is not a requirement, and treating it as one is how this stage stops being bearable.
>
> After: It's okay to stop. Having sex every two days through the middle of your cycle covers your fertile days
> without a single strip, chart or reminder. Tracking helps if your cycles are irregular or you'd like notes for a
> doctor. It isn't a must, and it shouldn't make this time harder than it already is.

**7. A daily card**
> Before: An egg lives for about a day after it is released. Sperm can survive around five days inside the body.
> That means the days BEFORE ovulation matter as much as the day itself - which is why ParentVeda shows a window of
> several days rather than pointing at one.
>
> After: An egg lives for about a day after it's released, but sperm can survive around five days inside the body.
> So the days before ovulation count just as much as the day itself. That's why we show you a window of several
> days instead of one date.

**8. Help text inside a tool** (symptom log)
> Before: Tap whatever fits. Nothing is required, and there is no wrong answer — a few cycles of this is what turns
> "I think this happens sometimes" into something you can show a doctor.
>
> After: Tap whatever fits today. Nothing is required and there's no wrong answer. After a few cycles, "I think this
> happens sometimes" becomes something you can show your doctor.

**9. A disclaimer** (symptom log)
> Before: This records what you noticed. It never interprets it, and it is never a diagnosis. In the two weeks
> before a period is due, early pregnancy and an approaching period feel identical — they are the same hormone — so
> nothing here can tell you which it is.
>
> After: This keeps a note of what you felt. It doesn't read anything into it, and it's never a diagnosis. In the
> two weeks before your period, early pregnancy and a period on its way can feel exactly the same, because the same
> hormone is behind both. So nothing here can tell you which one it is.

**10. A screen with missing data** (cycle chart)
> Before: There is a gap in what has been logged, so putting fertile days on this chart would be a guess. Your days
> are all still here — the colours come back once a couple of cycles are recorded.
>
> After: A few days are missing from your log, so marking fertile days here would only be a guess. Everything
> you've logged is safe. The colours come back once a couple of cycles are recorded.

**11. A door tile** (tiles are mostly fine already; the fix is who we are talking to)
> Before: Which days can she get pregnant? · About six days each cycle. Here is why.
>
> After: Which days can you get pregnant? · About six days each cycle. Here's why.

## 6. How each rewrite is checked

1. Read against this guide: rules, banned list, no dashes.
2. Facts compared line by line with the original: every number, dose, timing and warning sign still there.
3. Ids and keys unchanged. `flutter analyze` clean, and the full test suite passes.
4. The competitor check: no run of 8 or more words in a row matching Flo or What to Expect text.
5. Walked on the phone before it is committed.

## 7. For the helpers doing the rewrite (technical notes)

- Change only the English text inside strings. In `_t(en, hi)` and `_p(en, hi)` pairs, change the first string only.
  `titleHi`, `bodyHi` and similar fields stay as they are.
- Contractions add apostrophes. In a single-quoted Dart string, either switch that string to double quotes or
  escape the apostrophe (`it\'s`). Run `flutter analyze` after every file.
- Leave code comments alone. They are for developers, not for her.
- If you are not sure whether a string is shown to her or used by the code (compared, saved, a map key), search
  where it is used before changing it. When still unsure, leave it and list it.
- Titles may change (the `id` never does). Tests that check exact text are updated only where the text change was
  intended. Safety tests are never weakened.
